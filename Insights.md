## Business insights For Duka Smart Electronics#**

**Data:** (150 customers, 800 orders, Duration:January 2025 – September 2026)
**Rules used:** revenue = `quantity * unit_price * (1 - discount / 100)`, cost = `quantity * cost_price`,
profit = revenue − cost. All money figures use **Completed orders only**; order counts include every order.
Total completed revenue is about **KSh 24.2 million** from 132 paying customers.

## Insight 1 — Five products bring in more than half of all revenue
*Evidence: Q46(b), Q18*

**Finding:** The top five products earned KSh 12,528,777 between them, about **52% of all completed
revenue** (Q46b). The iPhone 13 alone brought in KSh 3,766,458.15 from just 45 units, followed by the
HP 250 G9 Laptop (KSh 2,510,510.15), Hotpoint Refrigerator (KSh 2,386,956.60), Lenovo IdeaPad 3
(KSh 2,032,875) and Samsung 55in Smart TV (KSh 1,831,977.10). These five products sold only 195 units
in total.

**Why it matters:** Half the business rests on a few high-priced items. If one of them goes out of stock, has a supplier price rise, or loses ground to a competitor's offer, revenue drops sharply.
And because each sale is large, losing even a handful of orders a month makes a visible difference.

**Recommendation:** Treat these five products as "never out of stock" items, with reorder alerts and a backup supplier for each. Sell accessories alongside them (cases, screen protectors, laptop bags,
surge protectors), so every big-ticket sale also earns extra margin.


## Insight 2 — Mobile Phones is the most profitable category, but keeps only about 10 shillings in every 100
*Evidence: Q33, Q46(c), Q42*

**Finding:** Mobile Phones earned the most profit of any category: KSh 728,760.65 on revenue of KSh 6,942,060.65 (Q46c). That is about **29% of all company revenue**. But its profit margin is
**only 10.5%**: for every KSh 100 of phones sold, KSh 89.50 goes on the cost of the phone. More than half of the category's revenue (54%) comes from one model, the iPhone 13.

**Why it matters:** Phones win on volume, not on margin. With margins this thin, a small increase in discounts or supplier prices can wipe out most of the profit. For example, an extra 5% discount on phones
would remove almost half of the category's profit.

**Recommendation:** Hold back on further phone discounts. Instead, attract phone buyers with free add-ons that cost the company little, such as a screen protector or a case. Negotiate volume pricing with the
iPhone supplier, since that one model carries the category. Compare the 10.5% margin against the other categories in Q33, and give more shop-front space to any category with a better margin.


## Insight 3 — The biggest spenders are labelled "Retail", not VIP
*Evidence: Q45, Q46(a)*

**Finding:** All five of the highest-spending customers are in the **Retail** segment, not VIP or
Corporate (Q46a). Brian Chebet (KSh 848,118.75), Peter Ndungu (KSh 790,229.50), Lucy Njoroge
(KSh 767,880.80), Nancy Korir (KSh 702,545.75) and Purity Ochieng (KSh 698,921.60) together spent KSh 3.8 million, about 16% of all revenue. Brian Chebet alone spent more than nine times the median
customer (KSh 89,999).

**Why it matters:** The customer segment labels do not reflect how customers actually behave. If VIP offers and service go only to customers labelled VIP, the company is ignoring the people who spend the
most. These customers could easily be won over by a competitor that treats them better.

**Recommendation:** Re-segment customers based on what they spend, using the Q45 value bands, rather than the label they were given when they registered. As a first step, invite the five customers above,
and any other Retail customer in the High Value band, to the VIP programme. After that, review segments
every quarter.


## Insight 4 — Ten customers account for almost a third of revenue
*Evidence: Q24, Q41, Q45*

**Finding:** Of 132 paying customers, the top 10 (under 8% of buyers) spent KSh 7,187,146.55 between them, which is **29.75% of all completed revenue** (Q24). On average each of them spent about KSh 718,700, roughly eight times the median customer. Spending varies enormously, from KSh 1,899 to KSh 848,118.75. A customer at the 75th percentile spends KSh 252,835, about seven times one at the 25th percentile
(KSh 35,201) (Q45). Another 18 registered customers have no completed purchase at all.

**Why it matters:** The business depends heavily on a handful of relationships. Losing two or three top customers would cost more than losing dozens of small ones. Management would not notice that
risk if it only looked at total sales.

**Recommendation:** Treat the top 10 customers as key accounts, with a named contact at the branch, early access to new stock, and a quarterly check-in call. At the same time, reduce this dependence by helping
Medium Value customers (KSh 35,201–252,835) move up a band through bundles and loyalty pricing. Send the 18
non-buyers a first-order offer.


## Insight 5 — January is the weakest month, and January 2026 was worse than January 2025
*Evidence: Q43, Q46(d), Q40*

**Finding:** January 2026 was the **lowest-revenue month** of the 21 months in the data, at only KSh 322,432.95 (Q46d). That is **26% below January 2025** (KSh 434,902.70), even though more orders were placed (25 against 11). Revenue per order placed fell from about KSh 39,500 to about KSh 12,900. In other words, customers were still coming in but buying much cheaper items. Margins also swing widely from month to month: from 4.6% in January 2025 to 14.3% in February 2025 (Q43).

**Why it matters:** January is when Kenyan households are paying school fees after the December holidays, so
spending on electronics drops. The large fall in order value suggests customers switch to accessories and
put off big purchases. The swing in monthly margins shows that discounting is not being controlled
consistently.

**Recommendation:** Plan for January rather than react to it. Offer instalment payment or "pay later via M-Pesa" deals on laptops and phones for back-to-school buyers, and keep heavy promotions for the slow months rather than the busy ones. Set a minimum margin target for every month
(for example, no lower than 10%), and check discounts against it before approving promotions.

## Summary for management

1. **Protect the top five products.** They bring in about half of revenue, so keep them in stock and sell accessories with them.
2. **Stop discounting phones.** At a 10.5% margin, discounts eat most of the profit, so use free add-ons instead.
3. **Re-segment customers by spending.** The top spenders are Retail customers being treated as ordinary buyers.
4. **Look after the top 10 customers** and grow the Medium Value band to reduce dependence on them.
5. **Plan for January** with back-to-school instalment offers, and set a monthly minimum margin.
